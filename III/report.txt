1. Introduction
PizzaHub, a major pizza delivery service, operates across four regions with varying distances, costs, and customer demand levels. On March 1, 2023, the firm introduced targeted discounts in two treatment regions:
Region 3: 5% discount on the food fee


Region 4: 5% discount on the delivery fee


Regions 1 and 2 received no discount, serving as natural control groups.
2. Objective
Evaluate the causal impact of the two pricing interventions and recommend profit-maximizing pricing strategies for Regions 3 and 4.
Research Questions:
What is the causal effect of targeted food vs. delivery discounts on pizza sales in the respective regions?
Which pricing strategy is more effective at increasing the quantity sold?
What are the implications for future pricing and profit maximization?
How did the discounts affect pizza demand in Regions 3 and 4?

5. Methodology


5.1 Demand estimation (price and delivery fees)
We first estimate a reduced-form demand function at the daily region level using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). The dependent variable is Quantity Ordered, and the key explanatory variables are Unit Price and Delivery Fees, with region and date controls:
Qit=α+β1Priceit+β2DeliveryFeeit+μi+λt+εit
where i indexes region, t indexes date, μi are region fixed effects (implemented via region dummies), and λt are date fixed effects (implemented via date dummies). This specification allows us to recover the price sensitivity and delivery-fee sensitivity of demand while controlling for unobserved region characteristics and common daily shocks.

Parallel trends and robustness checks
So far in this report, we have visually inspected pre-treatment trends by plotting region-level daily demand (with 7-day rolling averages) prior to March 1st. The pre-trends for Regions 1–4 appear approximately parallel.

We run placebo DiD tests using only pre-period data and a fake treatment date of 1 February 2023. The placebo treatment effects are small and statistically insignificant for both Region 3 and Region 4, supporting the parallel trends assumption.


5.3 Profit estimation and multi-armed bandit optimisation
To link demand to profitability, we construct an approximate profit per order measure using an assumed unit cost of $4 per pizza:
Profit per order = (Unit Price−4) * Quantity Ordered
Regions 1 & 2: no_discount


Region 3: price_5pct (5% food discount from March 1)


Region 4: delivery_5pct (5% delivery fee discount from March 1).


We summarise average and total profit per order by strategy and then simulate four multi-armed bandit (MAB) algorithms, N-Average, ε-Greedy, UCB1, and Thompson Sampling, where each “arm” is a pricing strategy. In each round, the algorithm chooses a strategy, receives a profit-per-order reward sampled from the historical distribution of that arm, and updates its beliefs. This allows us to study which pricing strategy a profit-maximising learning algorithm would converge to.
6. Results
6.1 Demand estimation results
 The baseline OLS demand model (without date fixed effects) shows that both product price and delivery fees have strong negative effects on demand:
A $1 increase in Unit Price is associated with approximately 7.7 fewer pizzas per day (coefficient ≈ −7.73, p < 0.001).


A $1 increase in Delivery Fees is associated with approximately 8.7 fewer pizzas per day (coefficient ≈ −8.73, p < 0.001).


When we add date fixed effects (region and date dummies), the results are very similar and slightly stronger in magnitude:
Unit Price coefficient ≈ −8.41 (p < 0.001).


Delivery Fees coefficient ≈ −10.19 (p < 0.001).


This confirms that both higher food prices and higher delivery fees significantly reduce daily demand, even after controlling for unobserved time and region effects.
6.2 DiD treatment effects of discounts
The DiD models quantify the causal impact of the March 1 discounts on daily quantity ordered. In the joint model (Model 3 with region and date fixed effects), we obtain:
Food price discount in Region 3 (DID_R3)
Coefficient: +6.73 pizzas/day (p < 0.001)
Interpretation: after the 5% food price discount, Region 3’s daily demand increased by about 6.7 pizzas per day relative to what would have happened in the absence of the discount, using Regions 1 and 2 as controls.


Delivery fee discount in Region 4 (DID_R4)
Coefficient: +3.82 pizzas/day (p < 0.001)
Interpretation: the 5% delivery fee discount increased Region 4’s daily demand by about 3.8 pizzas per day, again relative to the control regions.


The common time effect (post) is statistically insignificant, implying that overall demand in the control regions is roughly flat across the pre- and post-periods.


Pre-trend plots and placebo DiD tests (using a fake treatment date of 1 February 2023) show no significant “fake” treatment effects, supporting the parallel trends assumption.

We then simulate four multi-armed bandit algorithms (N-Average, ε-Greedy, UCB1, and Thompson Sampling) where each arm represents one of the three pricing strategies. Across 2,000 simulated rounds, all policies achieve similar cumulative profits, but:
UCB1 and Thompson Sampling achieve the highest cumulative simulated profit, and all learning algorithms tend to concentrate their choices on the no-discount strategy, as it has the highest observed profit per order in the historical data.


In summary, discounts clearly increase demand (especially food discounts), but they reduce profit per order relative to no-discount pricing.
7. Managerial Recommendations
The DiD analysis shows that the 5% food discount in Region 3 increases demand by about 6.7 pizzas/day, while the 5% delivery discount in Region 4 increases demand by about 3.8 pizzas/day. Food discounts are therefore more effective at boosting volume.

However, the profit analysis indicates that the average profit per order is highest under the no-discount strategy, followed by the food discount, with the delivery discount being the least profitable.
Therefore, based on these findings, we recommend the following:
If the primary objective is profit maximisation:


Move Regions 3 and 4 towards the no-discount price over time, as this strategy yields the highest profit per order in the historical data.
In the short run, prioritise scaling back the delivery discount in Region 4, which has the weakest demand response and lowest profit per order.


But if PizzaHub prioritises demand growth or market penetration:


Use food (product) discounts rather than delivery fee discounts. The 5% food discount in Region 3 delivers almost twice the demand uplift of the 5% delivery discount in Region 4.


Consider time-limited or targeted food discounts (e.g., off-peak days or new-customer promotions) to boost demand while limiting the profit hit.


Algorithmic pricing and continuous learning:


Our bandit simulations show that simple learning algorithms (UCB1, Thompson Sampling) quickly identify the most profitable pricing strategy from historical data. PizzaHub could implement data-driven experimentation, using similar algorithms to continually test and adapt pricing in new regions.


Region-specific pricing strategy:


For high-delivery-cost regions like Region 4, delivery fee discounts are relatively inefficient: they raise demand only modestly and significantly reduce profit per order. If a discount is required, a small product discount combined with a transparent but higher delivery fee may be preferable.
9. Conclusion
PizzaHub’s targeted discount strategy was successful at increasing demand, especially in Region 3. The DiD approach provides strong causal evidence that food discounts have a greater effect on demand than delivery discounts, with an estimated uplift of +6.73 vs +3.82 pizzas per day respectively.

At the same time, our profit analysis shows that no-discount pricing currently yields the highest profit per order, with food discounts being less profitable and delivery discounts the least profitable of the three strategies. This highlights an important trade-off for PizzaHub: discounts are effective tools for demand generation and market penetration, but they come at a cost to per-order profitability.
Overall, PizzaHub should use food-based discounts strategically and selectively, especially in new or competitive regions, while relying on data-driven methods (like the multi-armed bandit framework used here) to continually evaluate whether the incremental demand from discounts justifies the associated profit sacrifice.
